For decades, China bought influence the loud way. Loans. Railways. Megaprojects. But debt distress and backlash cost Beijing too, so the playbook changed. The new lever is price. State-backed firms, armed with subsidies and overcapacity, arrive cheaper, take market share, and embed themselves. Nowhere is this clearer than in Thailand: a hedge around the Malacca chokepoint, a manufacturing hub, and home to the largest overseas Chinese community. In 2025, China and Hong Kong made up thirty-one percent of foreign investment applications. And in July, Prime Minister Anutin's China visit produced over fifteen cooperation agreements. Start with cars. In the Detroit of Asia, Japanese brands once held over eighty-five percent of the market. Today, Chinese makers control more than seventy percent of the Thai EV market, helped by a price edge of almost twenty-nine percent. Then A.I. As Western models get pricier, cheap Chinese alternatives move in, and Chinese-linked capital is fuelling the data-centre boom. But Bangkok has pushed back. It stayed out of China's new A.I. body. It moved to drop the land bridge. It taxed cheap parcels. Yet the real contest is standards. A charging plug can be fixed with a dongle. A data architecture cannot. If embeddedness can constrain Thailand, it will constrain everyone else. Sooner, and with less warning. The Lever Is Price. A special report from the Observer Research Foundation.
