For decades, China built its influence abroad through loans, railways and megaprojects. But many left host countries in debt, stirred public backlash, and cost Beijing too. So the playbook changed. The new lever is price. State-backed companies now arrive with cheaper goods, powered by subsidies and overcapacity. They win market share, and become part of the host economy.

Nowhere is this clearer than in Thailand: a way around the Malacca chokepoint, a strong manufacturing base, and the largest overseas Chinese community. In 2025, China and Hong Kong made up thirty-one percent of its foreign investment applications.

Take cars. Japanese brands once held over eighty-five percent of the market. Today, Chinese makers control more than seventy percent of Thailand's EV market, with a price edge of almost twenty-nine percent. AI is following the same path, with cheaper Chinese models, and Chinese-linked money behind the data-centre boom.

Bangkok has pushed back. It stayed out of China's new AI body, moved to drop the land bridge, and taxed cheap imported parcels. But the real contest is over standards. A charging plug can be fixed with an adapter. A data architecture cannot.

If embeddedness can limit Thailand's choices, it can limit everyone else's too: sooner, and with less warning.
